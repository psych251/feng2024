# Chapter 4: Ethics (Experimentology, Chapter 4)

## Core ideas
- Deontology (duties that hold regardless of outcome) is the approach ethics boards take, alongside consequentialism (weigh outcomes) and virtue ethics (act as a person of good character would). Four principles govern research: respect for autonomy (informed consent and debriefing), beneficence (minimize and disclose risk, maximize benefit), nonmaleficence (do no harm), and justice (risks and benefits distributed equitably; no condition assignment based on identity) (§4.1.1 Consequentialist theories; §4.1.2 Deontological approaches; §4.1.3 Virtue-based approaches).
- Ethical research needs transparency and external oversight. The Belmont Report established that investigators cannot be sole judges of their own research; ethics boards check minimal risk, informed consent, confidentiality, and equitable, noncoercive recruitment before any data collection (§4.2 Ethical responsibilities to research participants; §4.2.1 Institutional review boards).
- Risks and benefits must be disclosed before consent and assessed from the participant's perspective; introducing a new procedure mid-study is coercive, and deception is a technical term requiring special consideration (§4.2.2 Risks and benefits).
- Researchers owe duties in analysis and reporting: avoid errors, never let the result decide whether a finding is reported, write carefully, and never plagiarize (including self-plagiarism). Fraud is the endpoint of a continuum that includes sloppiness and questionable practices (§4.3 Ethical responsibilities in analysis and reporting).
- Openness is a duty to the community, funders, and participants: sharing papers, materials, data, and code makes Merton's norms possible, and low-cost "incentive-compatible" openness (posting code, accepted manuscripts) should be the default (§4.4 Ethical responsibilities to the scientific community).

## Review checklist
1. Does the repository or writeup document ethics board approval or exemption obtained before data collection? (§4.2.1 Institutional review boards)
2. Does the experiment show a consent page disclosing purpose, procedure, duration, compensation, and all known risks and benefits before any data are collected, with benefits framed for the participant rather than the researcher? (§4.2.1; §4.2.2 Risks and benefits)
3. Can participants withdraw at any point without forfeiting expected compensation, and does the code avoid forced progression that undermines that right? (§4.1.2 Deontological approaches; §4.2.2 Risks and benefits)
4. Is the study minimal risk (no more discomfort than everyday life), and is any potentially distressing element disclosed and screened for? (§4.2.1 Institutional review boards)
5. Does the experiment end with a debriefing page giving participants context about the study? (§4.1.2 Deontological approaches)
6. If the study withholds goals, uses a cover story, under- or overstates aspects, or gives false information, is this identified as deception, justified, and revealed in the debrief? (§4.2.2 Risks and benefits)
7. Is condition assignment randomized in code, never tied to participant characteristics? (§4.1.2 Deontological approaches)
8. Are recruitment and compensation (e.g., Prolific eligibility and pay) equitable and noncoercive, without undue inducement or targeting of vulnerable populations? (§4.2.1 Institutional review boards)
9. Does the pipeline protect confidentiality: no Prolific IDs, IP addresses, or other identifiers in shared data or committed files? (§4.2.1 Institutional review boards)
10. Do the script and writeup show care to avoid errors and misstatements, with claims that match the analysis and no over-broad conclusions? (§4.3 Ethical responsibilities in analysis and reporting)
11. Are all preregistered analyses and outcomes reported regardless of significance or agreement with the hypothesis? (§4.3 Ethical responsibilities in analysis and reporting)
12. Is the writeup text original or attributed, with no unattributed copying from the original paper or other sources (reusing your own preregistration text is fine)? (§4.3 Ethical responsibilities in analysis and reporting)
13. Are code, materials, and (privacy permitting) data shared openly, given that code sharing is nearly always possible even when data sharing is restricted? (§4.4 Ethical responsibilities to the scientific community)

## Common mistakes the book warns about
- Undermining the right to withdraw through pressure to continue or by making opting out cost the participant their compensation ("Shock treatment" case study; §4.2.2 Risks and benefits).
- Springing an unexpected new component on participants mid-session, which is coercive and feels like deception (§4.2.2 Risks and benefits).
- Failing to screen for or minimize foreseeable harm, and not adapting or stopping when participants show distress (§4.1.2 Deontological approaches; §4.1.3 Virtue-based approaches).
- Recruiting coercively or inequitably from vulnerable populations, or with misleading promises ("The Tuskegee Syphilis Study" case study; §4.2.1).
- Suppressing findings because they contradict your theory or fall on the wrong side of p < 0.05 (§4.3 Ethical responsibilities in analysis and reporting).
- Sloppy data management, analysis, or writing that yields errors, imprecise claims, or irreproducibility ("What data?" accident report; §4.3).

## Key terms
- Deontological ethics: duty-based; actions must be permissible regardless of outcome; intent matters.
- Respect for autonomy: participants make informed decisions (consent) and receive context afterward (debriefing).
- Beneficence / nonmaleficence: minimize and disclose risks, maximize benefits; do no harm.
- Minimal risk: anticipated harm or discomfort no greater than everyday life; more requires additional monitoring.
- Informed consent: disclosure of all potential risks and benefits before data collection, ongoing if new ones emerge.
- Deception: withholding goals, hiding experimenter identity, under/overstating aspects of the study, or giving false or misleading information.
- Coercion: pressure that undermines voluntary participation or the right to withdraw.
- Plagiarism: copying text without attribution, including self-plagiarism (credit twice for the same product).
